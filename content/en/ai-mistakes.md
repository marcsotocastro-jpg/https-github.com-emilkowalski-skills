Artificial intelligence saves a huge amount of time, but it can also make you look bad if you use it carelessly. Nearly every problem comes from the same nine mistakes. None of them is technical: they're habits. Here's each one, why it happens and what to do instead, with prompts ready to copy.

## 1. Believing everything it says

AI assistants generate text that sounds convincing, but they don't check facts the way a person would. Sometimes they invent figures, dates, quotes, laws or links. This is known as "hallucination", and it happens more often when you ask about things that are very specific, very recent or not well known.

**What to do:** treat every figure, name or reference as something you need to check. For important facts, use a tool that cites its sources and open those sources. You can also ask the AI to review its own answer:

```prompt
Review your previous answer. List every statement that contains specific facts (figures, dates, names, laws) and give your confidence level for each: high, medium or low. Mark which ones I should verify with an official source.
```

## 2. Pasting private information

Passwords, card numbers, medical reports, contracts or customer data shouldn't end up in an AI chat. Depending on the tool and its settings, what you type may be stored and, in some cases, used to improve the service.

**What to do:** anonymise before pasting. Replace names with "Client A" and remove ID numbers, addresses and sensitive figures. The full list is in [what you should never share with an AI](guia:privacy).

## 3. Asking without context

"Write an Instagram post" produces generic text that could belong to anyone. The AI doesn't know what you do, who follows you or how you talk.

**What to do:** give context the way you would to a new colleague: who you are, who you're speaking to, what you want to achieve and the tone you use. The full formula is in [how to write a prompt that works](guia:prompt).

## 4. Settling for the first answer

The first answer is a draft. Many people copy it as is or give up because they "didn't like it".

**What to do:** have a conversation. Ask for specific changes: shorter, warmer, no clichés, with an example, as a table. Three or four rounds of tweaks turn an average text into a very good one.

## 5. Publishing text that sounds like AI

Some phrases give away unedited AI text: grand introductions, lists of three adjectives, lines like "in today's fast-paced world" or "it's important to note". Readers spot them more and more, and they cost you trust.

**What to do:** ask for a specific style and always read it aloud before publishing.

```prompt
Rewrite this text so it sounds natural, the way a person would write it. Remove clichés, long introductions and unnecessary adjectives. Use short sentences. Keep all the information.

[paste your text]
```

## 6. Using it for things it isn't good at

AI is excellent at drafting, summarising, explaining and generating ideas. It's less reliable with long calculations, breaking news, very specific local information (a shop's hours or prices) or decisions that depend on your personal situation.

**What to do:** use it to understand a topic and prepare questions, not as a replacement for a doctor, lawyer or tax adviser. For calculations, ask it to explain the method or give you the formula to do it in a spreadsheet.

## 7. Mixing topics in the same chat

If one conversation covers a meal plan, then your CV, then an email, the AI carries over context that no longer helps and can mix things up.

**What to do:** start a new conversation for each different task. Give it a clear name if the tool allows, so you can find it later.

## 8. Expecting it to do everything at once

Asking for "my complete business plan" usually produces something shallow. Big tasks come out better in pieces.

**What to do:** split it up. First ask for an outline, then develop each section separately and review each one before moving on.

```prompt
I want to prepare [big task]. Don't do it yet. First, suggest an outline with the sections needed and one line explaining what goes in each. Then we'll work through it section by section.
```

## 9. Not saying you used AI when it matters

Schools, universities and some workplaces have rules about using AI. Handing in AI-generated work as your own can have serious consequences.

**What to do:** check the rules of your school or company. Use AI to learn, summarise and correct, and write the final version yourself. There are ideas in [how to study with AI without cheating](guia:study).

> **Tip:** a good rule of thumb: let AI do the first 80% of the work and add the final 20% yourself, with judgement, review and real facts. That 20% is what makes the result yours, and good.

## Quick checklist before using a result

- Have I checked the important figures, names and dates?
- Did I remove private data from what I pasted?
- Does it sound like me, or like a template?
- Does it follow my school's or company's rules?
- Have I read the whole thing before sending it?

## FAQ

### Why does AI make things up?

Because it generates the most likely text based on patterns rather than looking up a database of verified facts. When it lacks reliable information, it can produce something that sounds right but is false. That's why it's worth asking for sources and checking them.

### How do I know if an answer is correct?

Look the fact up in an official or trusted source, ask the AI where the information comes from and compare with a second tool. If the answer matters, don't rely on a single source.

### Is it bad to use AI for writing?

No. It's a great help for getting started, organising ideas and proofreading. The problem is publishing without reviewing, or presenting unedited work as your own where the rules don't allow it.
